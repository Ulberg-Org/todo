It's how we turn untrusted input (here an app's `platform.yaml`) into domain values that are guaranteed valid. This is the "parse, don't validate" rule from the style guide. The block gives you three things at once:

1. **Either a fully valid value, or the complete list of what's wrong.** Nothing in between. Once you hold a `Service`, its replicas are ≥ 2, its port is a real port and its database exists. The renderer never has to check again, and can't accidentally work with a half-checked value.
2. **Every error in one go.** A developer who writes a broken `platform.yaml` sees all five problems in one run, not one per attempt. Writing that by hand means collecting errors into a list after every check, which is exactly the boilerplate the block removes.
3. **No exceptions, no hidden control flow.** Failures are ordinary values (`Error [...]`), so the pure part stays pure and trivially testable, and the program decides at the edge what to do with them: print and exit 1.

Without it, the usual alternatives are worse:
- **Throwing on the first bad field:** one error per run, and exceptions as control flow.
- **Hand-written if-chains appending to a list:** long, easy to get wrong, and the "is it valid yet?" state leaks out.
- **Checking fields where they're used, deep in the renderer:** validation is scattered, and invalid states can travel far before something notices.

The block is just the compact way to write "check these independent fields, give me all the problems, or give me the finished value".
